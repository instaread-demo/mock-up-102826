The impossible is becoming possible. Last week, Italian climber Pietro Vidi announced that he had made the second ascent of the world’s hardest trad climb: Drifter’s Escape (5.15a) in Squamish, British Columbia.

The 65-foot route was first climbed by Connor Herson in July 2025. Afterward, Herson kept his send a secret for seven months until he was ready to release a documentary about it. The achievement later earned Herson a Golden Piton.

The stats and significance of Drifter’s Escape

Drifter’s follows a series of parallel cracks through an otherwise blank headwall of white granite, with two V11 cruxes. The climb does include two bolts at the beginning, which makes it technically a mixed climb. But traditional gear protects the rest of the route, including the cruxes.

Local climber Ethan Salvo, who has projected the route with both Herson and Vidi, said the difficulty of Drifter’s primarily stems from “how punchy the whole upper half is.” Herson famously tackled the first V11 boulder problem by performing a “pogo.” As he bumped his right hand up,  he generated momentum by swinging out with his right leg—a sporty move typically seen in the gym, not on rock.

While the first ascent and first repeat of Drifter’s Escape mark a new era in hard trad climbing, both Herson and Vidi agreed that the discipline will continue to progress in terms of grades. “Trad is not even near the limit,” Vidi told Climbing. 

“Trad climbing has the most immediate potential for progression,” Herson shared with Climbing shortly after announcing his ascent in February. Both climbers also feel aligned that the primary challenge in pushing the limits of trad further is finding more hard trad lines.

Who is Pietro Vidi?

Vidi has been ticking hard lines for many years, but he gained particular notoriety in the spring of 2025, when he made the second free ascent of Lurking Fear (5.13c; 2000ft), one of the most popular aid lines on El Cap. Since Beth Rodden and Tommy Caldwell first freed the route in 2000, no climber had managed to free every pitch until Vidi ended the quarter-century dry spell.

Previously, Vidi divided his time between comp climbing and hard boulder problems and sport routes in Europe.

Last fall, he spent a fortnight ticking impressively hard routes in the UK Peak District, clocking:

  • 4 E9s (5.13/5.14 R/X) ascents
  • 2 highball boulders
  • 10 additional trad sends graded E7 6c (5.12+ R/X) or harder

The Peak District is known for bold routes on coarse gritstone. “I’ve always been fascinated by the Peak and its aesthetics,” Vidi told Climbing after his sending spree.

From England, Vidi headed back to Yosemite. And it was around that time when he heard about a line still on the down low: the world’s first 5.15 trad route. Currently, Vidi is in Yosemite again after completing Drifter’s Escape. From the top of El Cap, he answered our questions on his historic ascent, from what shoes he wore, to why he chose different beta than Herson.

Our Q&A with Pietro Vidi

This interview has been lightly edited for concision and clarity. 

Maya Silver: When and why did you decide you wanted to try Drifter’s Escape?

Pietro Vidi: I met Connor in [Yosemite] Valley last year. He told me about the thing he did before he posted and I saw some pictures. It looked like such a crazy line—so aesthetic and so beautiful. Camilla [Moroni], my girlfriend, and I decided to go to Squamish in the fall.

That really was the obvious goal of the year. I trained the whole summer, and got to Squamish in late-August, or maybe the first of September. I spent the first couple days on Dreamcatcher, then fully focused on Drifter’s. I eventually sent on October 12 in the afternoon.

Silver: How many times would you estimate you tried the route?

Vidi: Some sessions were just to find gear, so around 15. Maybe a bit more than 10 lead tries. I tried a lot on toprope just to figure out the moves and some better beta for me. Connor’s beta—especially the top move—was very, very hard for me. It was weird and not my style. I had to find my own beta. A lot of that was in September. I got very close at the beginning of October. Then it got warm and I had to wait for good conditions.

Silver: You noted that you placed less gear than Connor. What was your rack for your send go?

Vidi: My rack was brass nut 4, a #.3, three #.2s, two .2/.3s, and two .1/.2s.

Silver: You mentioned having to dial the toe jams in thin pods. What shoes did you wear to suit the style and why?

Vidi: I wore [Scarpa] Blackbirds. I tried it with Scarpa Boostics, but you need a pretty narrow shoe to fit in those pods and I really wanted very, very stiff shoes for the edging. [Editor’s note: Vidi is a sponsored athlete for Scarpa.]

Silver: How much beta did Connor share with you? Or did you focus on finding your own way up?

Vidi: I watched a video. Ethan Salvo was there with me for a lot of days and he tried a lot with Connor. He projected [it] for three seasons so he really helped me.

Connor’s and my style are very different. I’m a more physical climber. At the end and both of the crux sections, we maybe don’t even do one move the same way. The grade doesn’t change for sure. We had completely different beta. I climbed it in a more powerful way—or maybe more straightforward and quicker. I try to do all the routes I do with as few moves as possible, even if they are a bit harder.

Silver: Considering how relatively little time you seem to have invested in this project, what does this tell you about the limit of hard trad, both for you and within the sport?

Vidi: I did invest time. It was a whole month of trying. But I still think trad is very, very, very far from the limit. This might be a hot take, but a lot of sport climbers with one month of someone teaching them to place gear very well could repeat a lot of the hardest trad routes if they had the right headspace. For sure, trad is not even near the limit. But it’s so, so hard to find hard trad lines. They are so rare. It’s very hard to push the limit. That’s maybe something the newer generations—me and Connor—are trying to do. We’re seeing a cool evolution in the last few years.

Silver: Did you do the pogo?

Vidi: Actually no! I used some different feet. It’s super similar to Connor’s beta, just a bit more powerful on the pinch with two really bad feet. But that way, you don’t have to pogo. As I said, I don’t mind the moves to be harder if they save me some time or foot moves.
