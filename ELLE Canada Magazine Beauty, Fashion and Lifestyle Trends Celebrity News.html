'Project Runway Canada' Mentor Aurora James Is Making Fashion—and the World—a Brighter Place

"I still inherently believe that the world is a fairly magical place. I think that passion is contagious and that when you put certain things out into the world, [the universe] will conspire to help you make it so."

by : Katherine Lalancette-Oct 26th, 2026

Something special occurs when charged particles from the sun collide with atoms in the atmosphere. Scintillating ribbons of colour begin swaying spectacularly across the night sky, all because of disturbances in the earth’s magnetic field. It’s called an “aurora,” and it’s an image that feels particularly fitting for someone who’s been branded one of fashion’s top disrupters—a change maker who’s challenged norms and blasted barriers, igniting a trail of light in her wake.

Aurora James didn’t set out to revolutionize the fashion world; it just sort of…happened. Not that she didn’t work tirelessly to get where she is—quite the opposite—but there was no grand plan, no destination entered into the GPS.

Born in Guelph, Ont., to a Canadian mother and a Ghanaian father, she was raised by her mom and grandma mostly in Mississauga but spent a portion of her childhood in Jamaica. Though she inherited a deep appreciation for style from the matriarchs in her family, she never foresaw becoming a designer. The woman who would be named to the TIME100 list of most influential people in the world in 2021 dropped out of high school and was later kicked out of Ryerson (now Toronto Metropolitan) University. Unlike many of her peers in the industry, she didn’t attend a fancy fashion school or have an in at a major label. What she did have, though, was razor-sharp instincts and a propensity for action.

“A lot of the things I’m most commonly known for are spur-of-the-moment decisions that I executed on really quickly,” says the 42-year-old, reflecting on her journey. “It was like, ‘This excites me in my heart, so I want to go for it and I don’t have to think it through completely.’”

Case in point: In 2012, she noticed a guy in cool-looking lace-up boots. He informed her that they were veldskoens, traditional South African shoes colloquially called “vellies.” Before long, she was flying across the world in search of a workshop to partner with to update the design and bring it to the West. Brother Vellies was born. She launched the company in 2013, at 28, with $3,500 in savings and a goal of supporting artisans and proving that a shoe crafted in Africa could be just as covetable as one made in Milan. Two years later, the CFDA/Vogue Fashion Fund granted her US$300,000 to grow the business and big-name celebs like Beyoncé and Zendaya began rocking her designs.

Then, on May 29, 2020, four days after the murder of George Floyd at the hands of a white police officer, James hit “publish” on an Instagram post urging fashion and beauty behemoths to devote 15 percent (the relative size of the Black population in the U.S.) of their shelves to Black-owned businesses. The Fifteen Percent Pledge took off and transformed the retail space in a matter of months. Vogue splashed her portrait on that year’s September cover. Across it, the word “HOPE” boomed in bold type.

There is a fearlessness to James, a tendency to leap without glancing down. “I think sometimes the biggest failure is a failure to launch—to not even try,” she says. “People tend to be very nervous about regret, and I only get regretful about things I didn’t try.”

In town from Los Angeles, where she now resides, James is sitting in a Montreal restaurant on an early-September evening. She is swathed in an espresso Suzie Kondi terry set, which she’s paired with a vintage Greenpeace pin and a cheetah-print Chanel bag. Over the course of the meal, she shepherds the conversation from her love of gardening to Gloria Steinem’s legacy to her concern over recent AI cyberattacks to the importance of supporting small businesses.

For someone who was named after Sleeping Beauty (her mom had planned on calling her Zoe but changed her mind when her baby exited the womb dozing), she is acutely awake to the world, mainlining the news and perpetually looking to learn. Switch on your phone’s sound while admiring her bohemia-inflected outfit du jour in an Instastory and odds are a current-events podcast will be blaring in the background.

“I don’t think any of my friends could ever describe me without mentioning that I’m Canadian, because it’s such a core part of who all of us are.”

“As tempting as it is to just tune out—and a lot of us have had to, because the pandemic was really rough and I think we all have PTSD from that, and that’s part of why America’s been able to slip into this weird, dark, warm bath of craziness—I think we do have to be conscious of what’s going on around us, and we need to push for regulation and get involved at a local level.”

Over the years, James has teamed up with the Women’s March and Planned Parenthood, designed merch supporting Joe Biden and Kamala Harris and helped secure over US$10 billion in corporate commitments to Black-owned businesses. As she puts it, “What’s the point of having a seat at the proverbial table if you’re not going to use your voice?” The voice in question may have been characterized by some as “high-pitched” or “nasally” or “ditsy,” as James recounts in her 2023 memoir, Wildflower (she was once even advised to consult a vocal coach), but it has certainly found a way to reverberate.

While accepting her trophy for International Designer of the Year at the Canadian Arts & Fashion Awards in 2019, James touched on her concern for the greater good, attributing it to her upbringing. “Over and over again in America, I get asked the same question: ‘What makes you do this?’ And when they ask me, they’re honestly pensive about it. There is truly just one very clear answer: It’s the fact that I’m Canadian.”

“I don’t think any of my friends could ever describe me without mentioning that I’m Canadian, because it’s such a core part of who all of us are,” she says today. “There are little things that are a way of life here that are completely different in America. Health care is a huge one, because when you start there as a foundation, you are understanding that, essentially, we’re creating a society where one another’s well-being is table stakes. And that translates into a lot of other ways of being, day in and day out.”

She feels that it’s even informed her approach to design. “I think [Canadians] can appreciate something that’s culturally different without immediately feeling like they need to ‘other’ it or be threatened by it,” she explains. “I don’t know that I would have had the desire to seek out and learn about different cultures and ways of doing things and history if it weren’t for my background in Canada.”

It’s also part of what she believes makes Project Runway Canada unique. On the show (the second season of which will be coming to Crave in November), James coaches contestants as they battle for a $100,000 career-propelling investment, a feature spread in this very magazine and the title of Canada’s Next Great Designer. “If you look at our cast, it’s extremely diverse, and it just so happens to be that way because Canada is a diverse meritocracy,” she says. “That kaleidoscope, within the fashion context, can make for some beautiful, beautiful work. For me, that makes the design more interesting because there’s always more to it—there’s more under the hood.”

It’s clear that to James, fashion extends far beyond external adornment. It is at once art and personal expression, a mirror of mores and a vehicle for change. This is someone who grew up revelling in her mother’s treasure trove of a closet, which teemed with pieces from faraway places and decades past. She faithfully watched Jeanne Beker on Fashion Television, admiring how the journalist handled her subject matter with the fervour of a war correspondent.

In a fateful turn of events now part of Canadian fashion lore, James wound up meeting Beker while working as a receptionist in a Yorkville gym. Every day, she’d sit behind the front desk in her favourite vintage finds, and Beker, of course, would never fail to notice. “You are far too chic to be working at the gym,” she eventually told her, before going on to help her nab an internship on her show. It would be James’ first big break.

“She is truly a force—a real human firecracker,” Beker says of her former pupil. “The cogs are constantly turning with Aurora. She has so many great ideas, and she really cares about the big picture. It’s not just about fashion for fashion’s sake.”

Their paths have once again crossed with Project Runway Canada, on which Beker serves as a judge alongside supermodel and host Coco Rocha and designer Spencer Badu. “She’s really invested in these young designers,” Beker says of James’ mentorship style. “And she’s brutally honest, which is important.”

“I do care so much; it’s actually annoying,” James admits with a laugh. “I just spiral the whole time the runway is happening. A lot of times, I’m crying, I’m angry or I’m having big emotional reactions because it really matters to me; it’s someone’s future.”

Her participation in the show is somewhat of a full-circle moment, as she herself appeared in a televised competition while vying for the CFDA/Vogue Fashion Fund over a decade ago. “It’s a little trippy because I can relate to what they’re going through,” she says. “When I do hometown visits and we’re going to their house, it’s like, ‘Oh, my gosh, [I know that’s so] overwhelming.’ I remember when Anna Wintour came to my store [on the series].” James famously decided to rip up her shop’s laminate flooring the night before the Vogue editrix and camera crew were to arrive, revealing the raw concrete underneath. “She wouldn’t have cared about my floor, but you just freak out because there’s nothing else to do.”

James says she hopes that the show will help viewers recognize the wealth of talent that exists in this country and encourage people to shop more locally for their clothes. “I really want Canada to have a great sustainable fashion industry,” she says. “I wouldn’t be taking this much time out of my schedule and away from my other jobs to do this if it weren’t for that.”

She may flit around flea markets in floaty blouses and live in Laurel Canyon, but this free spirit is also one fiercely hard-working businesswoman. In the early days of the Pledge, she’d hold Zoom meetings from dusk ’til dawn, exhorting streams of CEOs to sign on, while also running her brand. There were many days when she survived on a mere three hours of shut-eye.

She’s barely slowed down since, relentlessly working, travelling and fundraising. (She also just added a new puppy—a chow chow, like her other two dogs—to the whirlwind because why not?)

“I guess I’m guilty of being from the toxic girlboss era,” she jokes, before becoming pensive. “I just feel so lucky to get to do so many of the things that I’m doing. I can wake up really, really tired, which I’m sure I will tomorrow, and I’ll be like: ‘Holy crap, I’m in Montreal. It’s September. It’s a beautiful freaking day, and I get to do something today that I’ve never done before.’ That’s what keeps me going.”

For all the doom and gloom in the headlines she consumes, her optimism shows no signs of waning. “I still inherently believe that the world is a fairly magical place,” she says. “I think that passion is contagious and that when you put certain things out into the world, [the universe] will conspire to help you make it so. I know that can feel a little bit Pollyannaish, but I think if you maintain a place of gratitude and you don’t forget where you came from and if you take tiny steps every day in the direction that you’re hoping to travel in, the distance adds up faster than you realize. And it’s never too late to turn around. It’s never too late to pivot.”

James herself chose to pivot recently. At the end of this past summer, she announced plans to take Brother Vellies back to its roots. Over the years, the company had diversified, offering all manner of fabulous footwear— from towering stilettos to thigh-high boots— which, although sold well, James didn’t feel as connected to. “I just want to take a beat and kind of go back to where I started, which was working with artisans and really getting my hands dirty,” she explains.

What else is on the horizon for her? She’s developed a keen interest in venture capital. She established a fund called the Friends and Family Collective and has invested in a number of companies, including Black-woman-owned makeup brand Danessa Myricks, emergency-contraceptive pill Julie and The Doux, a textured-hair-care line started by a Black couple with five kids.

“I’ve found a lot of fulfillment working with other founders and helping them grow their business,” she says. “I think you can invest in women and people of colour and perform just as well as if you were only investing in a bunch of white dudes who went to Ivy League schools.”

On the personal front, James reveals a desire to welcome a baby in the near future. She’s been candid on her Substack about the “non-traditional” bond she shares with entrepreneur and musician Benjamin Bronfman. “Imagine an ex-husband that lives next door” is how she described their relationship in a newsletter. The two sit down for meals daily and care for their dogs together. They also co-parent Bronfman’s teenage son (in fact, part of the reason she’s in town is to visit potential universities for him) and hope to one day expand their family.

“You don’t necessarily have to do the things your parents did or do things in the way you think you’re supposed to,” she says. “I have a desire to always reassess for myself what a win looks like and what a fail looks like, and it really has to be based on how I feel—me personally and not anybody else.”

James is making her own rules as she goes and feeling freer than ever for it. “We live just one time, from what I know,” she says with a cheeky smile. “So we gotta just do the thing.” Even when the thing is scary or disruptive or bound to clash with others’ beliefs. After all, sometimes clashes create the most dazzling light.
